Part 1: The Industry, Honestly

Software ate the world...

...and then AI showed up.

  • Every company is a software company now — banks, farms, hospitals, your school.
  • And as of the last couple years, AI coding tools are standard equipment. Every professional developer I know uses them daily.
  • This room needs to understand what that changed and what it didn't.

What AI changed

The easy code is free now.

  • Boilerplate, CRUD apps, simple scripts — seconds, not hours
  • "Write me a function that..." is a solved problem
  • Typing speed is no longer a skill that matters
If your entire skill set is "I can write a for loop," that skill just got commoditized. That's the bad news. The good news is next.

What AI didn't change

  • Someone has to decide what to build
  • Someone has to verify it's correct
  • Someone has to debug it when it's wrong at 2am
  • Someone owns it in production

Judgment is the job now.

  • AI is confident and wrong on a regular basis. It hallucinates APIs that don't exist.
  • The valuable person is the one who can look at AI output and say "this is right" or "this is subtly broken and here's why."
  • That takes real understanding. There's no shortcut around it.

The bar moved

2015: "can you write code?"

2026: "can you take a vague problem, ship something that works, and prove it works?"

Employers stopped testing whether you can write syntax. They test whether you can solve problems and stand behind the result. Tests, demos, explaining your reasoning — that's the interview now.

The junior market, honestly

  • More competitive than 5 years ago — I'm not going to lie to you
  • You're not competing against seniors
  • You're competing against other juniors...
  • ...and the ones using AI well have real leverage
  • Two years ago companies hired anyone who could fog a mirror and write JavaScript. That window closed.
  • But: the juniors who stand out now stand out MORE, because the average applicant is weaker — lots of people with tutorial certificates and no real projects.
  • Everything in part 2 is about being the applicant who stands out.

Where the demand actually is

  • Cybersecurity — always hiring, always will be
  • AI/ML-adjacent roles — data pipelines, evals, integration
  • Cloud & infrastructure
  • Data — every company is drowning in it
  • Boring business software. The unsexy stuff pays extremely well and nobody talks about it.
  • Everyone wants to build the next social app. Nobody wants to build the invoicing system for a plumbing company.
  • The plumbing company's invoicing system pays $120k and the founder will love you forever.
  • Follow the money, not the hype.

Remote work

Real. But:

  • Juniors get hired in-person / hybrid far more often
  • Your first job is probably local. That's fine.
  • Remote is something you earn with a track record
I work remotely now. My first jobs were in offices. Nobody hires a junior they've never met to work unsupervised — and honestly, you learn 10x faster sitting near experienced people.

Myths

Myth: "AI will take all the programming jobs"

It took the typing, not the thinking.

Compilers, open source, cloud — every wave made developers more valuable, not less.

When compilers arrived, people said assembly programmers were done. Instead, software ate everything and we needed 100x more developers. AI raises the floor — more people can build software, which means more software gets built, which means more developers needed to own it.

Myth: "You need a CS degree"

  • Helps for some doors. Opens zero doors by itself.
  • Portfolio + proof of work opens the rest.
  • When I hire, I look at what you built first.
A degree is a signal. "Here's a thing I built that real people use" is a louder signal. This room is at a technical school — you are already building the portfolio. Take that seriously.

Myth: "You need to be a math genius"

You need logical thinking.

Most dev jobs: arithmetic + logic + breaking big problems into small ones.

Unless you're doing graphics, ML research, or crypto, you will not touch calculus. What you need is the ability to hold a system in your head and reason about what happens when X changes. That's trainable.

Myth: "Learn every language"

Learn one deeply.

Languages are tools. Learn to think — the rest transfers in weeks.

I've written professionally in a dozen languages. The second one took a month, the third took two weeks. The hard part was learning the first one properly: data structures, control flow, how to structure a program. Pick Python or JavaScript and go deep.

Part 2: What Gets You Hired

Git + GitHub

Non-negotiable.

  • Your GitHub is your resume
  • Commit history proves you actually build things
  • Learn branching, pull requests, merge conflicts — this is how teams work
  • First thing I do with an applicant: open their GitHub. Empty profile with a resume claiming "passionate developer"? Next.
  • Green squares aren't the point — real projects with real commits are.
  • If you take one thing from today: push code every week.

One language, deeply

Python or JavaScript / TypeScript

Build real things. Not tutorials. Things you actually use.

Tutorial hell is real: you follow along, it works, you learned nothing. The learning happens when the tutorial ends and something breaks and nobody tells you why. Build a thing for yourself — a Discord bot, a budget tracker, a site for a club. Finish it. That's the whole game.

Linux command line

cd   ls   grep   ssh   git from the terminal

  • Servers run Linux. All of them.
  • This alone puts you ahead of half the applicants
Most juniors have never SSH'd into anything. If you can navigate a Linux box, read logs, and restart a service, you are already useful on day one. It's a weekend of practice and it pays off forever.

How the web actually works

  • HTTP — requests, responses, status codes
  • APIs — everything talks to everything via APIs now
  • JSON — the language data speaks
You don't need to memorize RFCs. But you should be able to explain what happens when you hit enter in a URL bar, and you should be comfortable calling an API and handling what comes back. That's 80% of modern development.

Basic SQL

Data lives in databases.

SELECT, JOIN, WHERE — a weekend of practice.

Every app you've ever used is a pretty face on a database. If you can write a JOIN without googling it, you're ahead of most bootcamp grads. SQLite is free and runs everywhere — no excuses.

Debugging

The #1 underrated skill.

  • Read the error message. All of it.
  • Bisect the problem — cut it in half until it's small
  • Explain it out loud (rubber duck works)
  • Senior devs aren't people who don't get errors. They're people who've seen 10,000 errors and have a system for the 10,001st.
  • The error message usually tells you exactly what's wrong. Juniors ignore it and guess. Don't guess — read.

Using AI without getting dumb

  • AI is a calculator for code — powerful, and wrong sometimes
  • Verify everything. Run it. Test it. Read it.
  • Never ship what you can't explain
  • If you can't explain it, you didn't learn it
  • The juniors who thrive with AI use it to go faster at things they understand. The ones who drown paste output they can't read and get stuck the moment it breaks.
  • Rule: if you couldn't write it yourself given enough time, don't ship the AI's version until you understand every line.

Writing

Docs. Commit messages. Emails. Questions.

The devs who write clearly get promoted faster. Every time.

Software is a team sport and teams run on writing. A clear commit message, a good bug report, a design doc people can actually follow — this is what separates mid-level from senior more than any technical skill. And it matters in interviews too: explain your thinking out loud, in order.

The boring ones that matter

  • Show up. On time. Every time.
  • Communicate — "I'm stuck" early beats "I'm stuck" late
  • Take feedback without ego
  • Do what you said you'd do
I have hired for attitude over skill more than once. Skills can be taught in months; reliability can't. The number of juniors who flake, go silent, or argue with feedback is staggering. Just... don't be that person and you're top 20%.

The Playbook

if I were 17 today

1. Build 3 real projects

Not tutorials. Things you actually use.

GitHub repos with READMEs that explain what and why.

Three finished projects beats thirty half-finished tutorials. "Finished" means: it works, there's a README, and someone other than you has used it. A README that explains the problem and your decisions is worth more than the code.

2. Contribute to open source

Even docs fixes count.

Proves you can work in a real codebase with real people.

Find a project you use, look at issues labeled "good first issue." Your first PR will be terrifying and your tenth will be routine. Hiring managers light up when they see real merged PRs — it means someone else already vetted you.

3. Learn in public

Write about what you build. Notes, blog, videos — pick one.

Hiring managers google you. Give them something to find.

You don't need an audience. Writing forces you to actually understand something — and a year of "here's what I built this month" is the strongest junior portfolio I've ever seen. It also proves the writing skill from earlier.

4. Get paid experience anywhere

  • Internship
  • School IT help
  • Freelance for a local business

"I shipped X for a real user" beats everything.

Doesn't have to be a tech company. The kid who built the scheduling spreadsheet-app for their uncle's shop has a better story than the kid with a 4.0 and no projects. Real users, real constraints, real deadlines.

About the interviews

What I'm listening for

  • How you think out loud
  • "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" — great answer
  • The questions you ask me
  • I'm not testing trivia. I'm testing: can this person reason through something unfamiliar without freezing?
  • "I don't know" is fine. "I don't know and I have no plan" is not.
  • Ask me questions at the end. "What's the hardest bug you ever fixed?" tells me you're curious. Curiosity is the whole job.
